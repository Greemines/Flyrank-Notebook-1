Ranking Content Pages for CTR Decline Review: A Grouped-Validation Comparison Against a Rule-Based Baseline

Abstract

A content team needs a way to prioritize which pages to review for declining search performance, but with thousands of pages and limited reviewer time, a simple rule for “what to check first” is not enough. This project used a 30,000-row dataset of anonymized content performance (32 clients, trailing-90-day metrics) to rank pages by their likelihood of being in decline, defined as trend_direction == "down". A rule-based baseline combining impressions, click-through rate, and average search position was compared against a Logistic Regression model trained on the same three signals, evaluated on a held-out split grouped by client so that no client appeared in both training and test data. Under this grouped split, the model reached 35.0% Precision@20 versus the baseline’s 30.0%, and both methods measured 44.0% Precision@50 — a modest, directional improvement at the top of the ranking rather than a broad gain. The result is offered as a decision-support ranking to guide human review, not as a causal or fully validated predictor of future page performance.

01 — Introduction

Introduction / Problem Statement

A content team managing thousands of pages across many clients cannot manually review every page for declining search performance. Some pages lose impressions, clicks, or ranking position over time, but with limited reviewer hours, someone has to decide which pages to look at first.

This project builds a ranking that supports that decision. Given a page’s recent search performance — how many impressions it received, its click-through rate, and its average search position — the goal is to estimate which pages are worth reviewing first for signs of decline, so that a content or SEO team can prioritize its limited attention.

Getting this wrong has a real cost. If the ranking sends reviewers to pages that are not actually declining, the team wastes time on false leads while pages that genuinely need attention go unreviewed. Because content review capacity is limited, a better ranking is one that puts genuinely declining pages nearer the top, even if it cannot catch every one.

A single fixed rule — for example, “flag any page with CTR below X” — struggles here because no one signal reliably separates declining pages from stable or growing ones on its own: a page can have a low CTR because it competes for an ambiguous query, not because it is declining, while a page with reasonable CTR can still be losing traffic. This project therefore compares a simple rule-based baseline against a learned model on the same signals, to see whether the extra complexity of a model earns its keep.

The result is intended as a triage tool for human reviewers, not an automated verdict. It ranks pages by estimated review priority; a person still decides what, if anything, to change.

02 — Data

Data

This project used the FlyRank internship starter dataset: content_refresh_anonymized.csv, 30,000 rows, one row per pseudonymized content page, drawn from 32 client accounts. Each row summarizes a page’s performance over a trailing 90-day window — impressions, clicks, click-through rate, average search position, engagement metrics, and content metadata (word count, content type, freshness, and similar fields).

All identifiers in the dataset — content_id and client_id — are pseudonymized. They were used only for grouping (for example, keeping all of a client’s pages together during train/test splitting), never as model features.

What was excluded, and why

  • trend_direction and trend_pct — these fields define the prediction target itself. Using them as features would leak the answer into the input.
  • Rate-style columns (ctr, engagement_rate, scroll_rate, ai_traffic_pct) required care because they are expressed as percentages that can behave unusually (for example, some ratio-based fields can exceed 100 because their numerator and denominator come from different measurement systems) — these quirks were accounted for during feature preparation rather than treated as errors.
  • Client and content identifiers were excluded from the feature set for the reasons above.

A separate, earlier exploration of FlyRank’s larger hosted warehouse (fact_content_daily_performance, using a single month partition for testing) was used only to practice querying and to build a data contract exercise. It uses a different schema (gsc_impressions, gsc_clicks, etc.) than the starter CSV and does not feed into the final model, baseline, or results reported in this paper.

No client names, private URLs, search queries, or other identifying business information appear anywhere in this dataset or in this paper.

03 — Methodology

Methodology

Task framing

This is a ranking problem, not a strict classification problem: the goal is to order pages from highest to lowest priority for review, not to declare each page “declining” or “not declining” in isolation.

Label definition

The target is derived from the trend_direction field: pages labeled "down" are treated as declining (target = 1); all other directions ("up", "stable", "flat", "new") are treated as not declining (target = 0). This label is a proxy — it summarizes an observed trend in the data, not a ground-truth judgment about why a page is declining.

Features

Both the baseline and the learned model use the same three signals:

  • impressions_90d — search visibility over the trailing 90 days
  • ctr — click-through rate
  • avg_position — average search ranking position

Keeping the feature set identical between baseline and model isolates what the model adds beyond the rule.

Baseline

The Week-4 baseline is a hand-written scoring rule, not a learned model: baseline_score = 0.50 × impression_score + 0.30 × ctr_score + 0.20 × position_score, where each component is normalized to bias score in favor of pages with higher visibility, lower CTR (more room to improve), and better search position. It assigns a single reason code, CTR_OPPORTUNITY, to every ranked page.

Model

The learned model is a Logistic Regression classifier trained on the three features above, with impressions_90d log-transformed (log1p) and all features standardized before fitting. Logistic Regression was chosen for its interpretability and because its output probabilities can be directly used to rank pages, which fits the ranking framing above and allows a direct comparison with the baseline using the same evaluation metric.

Validation design

Pages from the same client are correlated with each other (similar content strategy, similar traffic patterns), so a random train/test split risks letting the model “see” a client’s pages during training and then get evaluated on more pages from that same client — inflating the measured performance. To avoid this, the data was split using GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client_id, holding out approximately 20% of clients (not just pages) for testing. This produced 23,837 training rows across 25 clients and 6,163 test rows across 7 clients, with zero client overlap between the two sets.

A random 80/20 split was also tested for comparison (see Results). It produced 31 overlapping clients between train and test, which is why it is not used as the primary result.

Success metric

The primary metric is Precision@20: of the top 20 pages the ranking places at the front of the queue, what fraction are labeled declining. This metric was chosen because review capacity is limited — a content team is more likely to act on the top handful of recommendations than the full ranked list, so precision at the top of the list matters more than overall accuracy. Precision@50 is reported alongside it to check whether the pattern holds slightly further down the queue.

Leakage checks

The final feature set was audited against the target definition and against the full column list for the dataset. No identified direct target leakage was found in the final feature set: neither trend_direction nor trend_pct — the two fields the target is derived from — is used as a model feature. This audit checked the features actually used; it does not establish that every possible form of leakage in the dataset has been ruled out.

04 — Results

Results

Model vs. baseline, on the grouped-by-client test split

MethodPrecision@20Precision@50
Week-4 Baseline (rule)30.0%44.0%
Logistic Regression35.0%44.0%

On the held-out set of 7 clients the model never saw during training, the Logistic Regression model ranked declining pages into the top 20 more often than the rule-based baseline — 35.0% versus 30.0%. At the top 50, the two methods measured identically at 44.0%. This is a modest, directional improvement concentrated at the very top of the ranking, not a broad or dramatic gain across the whole queue.

Error analysis, top 20

Of the model’s top 20 ranked pages, 7 were labeled declining (correct) and 13 were not (false positives). The baseline’s top 20 contained 6 correctly labeled declining pages. Across the full test set, the model produced 1,723 false positives and 1,122 false negatives. This means most of the top-ranked pages, under either method, were not actually labeled declining — a high rank should be read as “worth reviewing,” not as a declaration that a page is failing.

Random vs. grouped validation

Random 80/2031 clients60.0%52.0%

A random 80/20 split of the same data measured a substantially higher Precision@20 (60.0%) than the grouped split (35.0%) — but 31 clients appeared in both the training and test sets under the random split. Because pages from the same client tend to share patterns, this overlap makes the random-split number an optimistic estimate rather than a fair test of generalization to new clients. The grouped-by-client result, with zero client overlap, is treated as the primary and more credible measurement in this paper. The random-split figure is reported here only to show why the grouped design was chosen, not as evidence of the model’s real-world performance.

05 — Limitations

Limitations & Honest Framing

The label is a proxy, not ground truth. “Declining” means trend_direction == "down" — a category computed from observed metric changes in this dataset. It is not an independent judgment of business impact, and it says nothing about why a page’s numbers moved.

The test set is small at the client level. The grouped validation held out 7 clients (6,163 rows). This is enough to compare methods directionally, but a 7-client test set is not enough to guarantee the measured precision would hold on a very different set of clients, industries, or content types.

The improvement is narrow. The model beat the baseline only at Precision@20 (35.0% vs. 30.0%); at Precision@50 the two were identical (44.0% vs. 44.0%). This should be read as a modest gain concentrated at the very top of the ranking, not a general-purpose improvement in review quality.

Most top-ranked pages are still false positives. In the model’s top 20, 13 of 20 pages were not labeled declining. Across the full test set, the model produced 1,723 false positives and 1,122 false negatives. A high rank is evidence worth checking, not a diagnosis.

Validation design changes the result substantially. The same model measured 60.0% Precision@20 under a random split and 35.0% under a grouped split. This gap shows that how a model is validated can matter as much as which model is used — and it means results from a different validation design (e.g., a time-based split, which was not tested here) could differ from both numbers reported in this paper.

The leakage audit is not exhaustive. The check confirmed that the two target-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct) are not used as features. It did not test every column in the dataset for subtler, indirect leakage paths.

Client history is uneven. Clients in this dataset differ in how much history and activity they contribute, so the model and baseline are implicitly weighted toward clients with more represented pages.

No causal claims are supported. This is a cross-sectional ranking built from historical metrics, not a controlled experiment. Nothing in this paper shows that changing a page’s title, content, or metadata causes a change in its ranking or traffic — the results describe correlation with an observed trend label, not a mechanism.

06 — Recommendations

Ranked Recommendations

These recommendations are a decision-support playbook for a content or SEO team, not an automated action list. Every recommendation requires a human reviewer before anything changes.

1. Use the model ranking to order review, not to filter it out

Pages near the top of the Logistic Regression ranking are candidates for earlier human review. A high score means “worth checking soon” — it does not mean “confirmed declining.” Given the false-positive rate observed in testing, a reviewer should expect roughly 2 out of every 3 top-ranked pages to turn out not to be declining.

2. Treat the ranking as a triage signal, paired with a quick manual check

For each ranked page, a reviewer should look at the same three signals the model uses — impressions, click-through rate, and average position — alongside the observed trend direction, before deciding whether the page needs deeper attention.

3. Flag disagreements for extra scrutiny

When the model ranks a page highly but the page’s own recent numbers don’t support a decline story (for example, trend_direction is "stable" or "up"), that mismatch is itself useful information — it suggests the ranking and the observed evidence disagree, and a reviewer should look closer rather than trust either signal blindly.

4. Periodically sample lower-ranked pages

Because the model misses a meaningful share of declining pages (1,122 false negatives measured in testing), a reviewer should not assume that pages outside the top of the queue are safe. Spot-checking further down the list helps catch what the ranking misses.

5. Do not treat “not flagged” as “healthy”

A low model score reflects the three signals used here, not a full picture of page health.

What should never be automated from this output
  • Automatically rewriting or publishing content
  • Automatically deleting or redirecting a page
  • Changing factual claims on a page without review
  • Declaring a page “definitely declining” based on the score alone
  • Claiming that a content refresh will improve performance
  • Treating a low score as proof a page is healthy
  • Making any irreversible content or SEO change without a human decision

The model produces a review queue. A person makes the final call.
